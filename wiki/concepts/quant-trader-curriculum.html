Quant Trader Curriculum — the knowledge center

Definition: A 6-module, ~24-week learning path that takes the operator from
"thematic investor with a data pipeline" to "systematic trader who can build, measure,
and retire signals." Every module ends in an artifact committed to this repo — you learn
by shipping code that touches real data, not by reading.

Scope. Skills education for the operator's own book. Nothing here is advice to others.
The syllabus is opinionated: it teaches the slow quant discipline (daily bars,
cross-sectional factors, risk sizing) because that is the only quant space a solo
operator can actually win in — see quant-firm-anatomy §1.

The skill ladder (what "good" looks like at each level)

Level You can… Evidence you're here
L0 Investor Form a thesis, size by conviction, track outcomes The vault today: decision-register, track-record
L1 Signal literate Compute a factor, z-score it cross-sectionally, plot its IC You can read scripts/qae.py §Factors and change it
L2 Researcher Design a falsifiable signal test that avoids look-ahead and survivorship You built a factor, measured IC, and killed it when t<2
L3 Portfolio builder Turn ranks into a risk-sized book with costs, caps, and stops Your book's realized vol ≈ your target vol
L4 Systematic operator Run a monthly loop: rescore → rebalance → attribute → retire 6+ months of attribution showing where P&L came from
L5 Alpha engineer Add genuinely new, weakly-correlated alphas that survive out-of-sample New factor raises composite IR, not just in-sample IC

Progression is gated by evidence, not by time. Do not advance on having read the module.

Module 1 — The mental model (week 1–2)

Goal: stop thinking in stories, start thinking in distributions.

Core ideas to internalize:
1. Expectancy, not accuracy. E = p·avg_win − (1−p)·avg_loss. A 35%-hit-rate system with
3:1 payoff beats a 65%-hit-rate system with 1:2. Most retail intuition optimizes the wrong term.
2. Variance is the constraint. Two strategies with identical CAGR and different vol are not
the same strategy — one is investable, the other is not survivable.
3. The Fundamental Law: IR ≈ IC × √Breadth (quant-firm-anatomy §3). Everything you do
is either raising IC or raising independent breadth.
4. Edge decays. Assume every signal you find has a half-life. Plan the replacement.
5. Costs are an alpha. Turnover × spread is a guaranteed negative return; it is the easiest
part of the P&L to improve.

Exercise 1.1 — Write down the expectancy of your last 20 real trades from
track-record: hit rate, average win, average loss, expectancy per trade. If expectancy is
positive but P&L is not, the gap is size/costs, not signal.

Exercise 1.2 — Compute your effective breadth: how many genuinely independent bets did
you make last year? (Hint: five AI-capex names is closer to one bet than five.)

Read: Grinold & Kahn Active Portfolio Management ch.1–6 · Ernie Chan Quantitative
Trading (whole book, it is short) · quant-firm-anatomy.

Module 2 — Data hygiene (week 3–4)

Goal: be able to prove your backtest is not lying to you.

The four killers, in order of how often they fool people:
| Bias | What it is | How to detect it |
|---|---|---|
| Look-ahead | Using data you could not have had at decision time | Shift every fundamental by its actual report lag; check that no factor uses t to predict t |
| Survivorship | Universe contains only names that still exist | Count delisted tickers in your history. If zero → you have it |
| Restatement | Fundamentals show the revised number | Requires point-in-time data (SEC EDGAR filings by filing date) |
| Overfitting | 200 backtests, report the best one | Track how many variants you tried; demand out-of-sample |

Exercise 2.1 — In this repo: scripts/_hist/prices.parquet has ~330 tickers. List which
ones stopped trading during the window and quantify the survivorship tilt (compare universe
mean return with and without the dead names).

Exercise 2.2 — Audit one factor in signals.py for look-ahead. Write the audit note into
the wiki. This is the single highest-value exercise in the whole curriculum.

Read: Marcos López de Prado Advances in Financial Machine Learning ch.1–7 (especially
the section on backtest overfitting and the deflated Sharpe ratio).

Module 3 — Factors and the IC discipline (week 5–9)

Goal: measure whether a signal predicts anything, honestly.

The workflow, which is exactly what scripts/qae.py implements:
1. Define the factor as a formula on data you had at time t.
2. Cross-sectionally z-score within a comparable group (stocks vs ETFs) and winsorize at ±3.
3. Compute rank IC = Spearman(signal at t, forward return t→t+h) for h ∈ {5, 21, 63} days.
4. Report IC mean, IC std, IR = mean/std, t-stat = mean/(std/√n), hit rate.
5. Decision rule: keep only if IC > 0 and |t| ≥ 2 across a decay curve that makes economic
sense. Reject a factor whose IC is high at 5d and negative at 63d unless you can trade at 5d
after costs.
6. Check correlation to existing factors. A new factor that correlates 0.8 with momentum is
not a new factor.

Benchmarks for sanity: a real, tradable equity factor has IC of roughly 0.02–0.06 at a
21-day horizon. IC > 0.10 on a broad universe means you have a bug (usually look-ahead)
until you prove otherwise (quant-firm-anatomy §2).

Exercise 3.1 — Run scripts/.venv/bin/python scripts/qae.py --no-backtest and read
scripts/_hist/qae_ic.json. For each factor, write one sentence on why it should work
economically. Any factor you cannot explain is a candidate for deletion.

Exercise 3.2 — Add one new factor to build_factors() (candidates: 52-week-high proximity
already there — try earnings-announcement drift, residual reversal, or downside-beta). Measure
its IC. Delete it if t < 2. Deleting your own idea on evidence is the actual skill.

Read: Jegadeesh & Titman (1993) momentum · Fama & French (2015) five-factor · Asness,
Frazzini & Pedersen (2014) Quality Minus Junk · Daniel & Moskowitz (2016) Momentum Crashes.
The vault already applies these in foresight-factor-model.

Module 4 — Portfolio construction (week 10–14)

Goal: turn a ranking into a book whose risk you actually control.

  1. Sizing. Inverse-volatility (w ∝ 1/σ) is the 80/20 of risk parity. Scale the whole
    book so ex-ante portfolio vol hits a target (10–15% annualized for a long book).
    Portfolio vol with n names, avg vol σ̄, avg correlation ρ:
    σ_p ≈ σ̄ · √(1/n + ρ(1−1/n)) — note it floors at σ̄√ρ no matter how many names you add.
    That floor is why diversification stops helping around 20–30 names in a correlated market.
  2. Constraints. Single name ≤ 8%, theme ≤ 12%, AI-capex cluster ≤ 25%, cash ≥ 3%
    (the portfolio-blueprint rules). Constraints beat optimizers for small books.
  3. Neutrality. Decide explicitly what you are not betting on. Beta-neutral? Theme-neutral?
    If you are not neutral to it, you are betting on it whether you meant to or not.
  4. Rebalancing. Bands (trade only when weight drifts >X%) cut turnover ~40% versus calendar
    rebalancing at similar returns (industry knowledge; verify on your own data).
  5. Costs. Model them or you will import fake alpha: cost = turnover × (spread/2 + fees).

Exercise 4.1 — Take today's QAE book and compute its ex-ante vol, beta, and theme
concentration. Does it breach any portfolio-blueprint cap? Fix it by constraint, not by
re-ranking.

Exercise 4.2 — Re-run the QAE backtest at 10, 20 and 40 names per leg. Plot Sharpe vs n.
Find where diversification stops paying for the added turnover.

Read: Grinold & Kahn ch.14 (portfolio construction) · Ang Asset Management ch.1–5.

Module 5 — Risk, stops, and the drawdown ladder (week 15–18)

Goal: pre-commit to how you lose, before you lose.

Adopt the pod-shop ladder, scaled to a personal book (quant-firm-anatomy §1):

Book drawdown from peak Action
−5% Review: is this factor decay or normal noise? Cut gross exposure 25%.
−8% Cut gross exposure 50%. No new positions outside the top-decile score.
−12% Flat the systematic book. Full post-mortem before re-entry. Re-enter at half size.

Per-position: stop at 2.5 × ATR(20) below entry — a volatility-scaled stop, so a 60%-vol
name gets a wider stop than a 15%-vol name and both risk the same dollars. A fixed-percentage
stop is a hidden bet that all your names are equally volatile.

Invalidation ≠ stop. A stop is a price event. An invalidation is a thesis event
(the score leaves the top quartile; the factor's IC turns negative; the theme's driver breaks).
Both must be written down before entry — this is already the vault's decision-register rule.

Exercise 5.1 — For each position in the current book, write the stop price AND the
invalidation condition. Any position where you cannot state the invalidation should not be on.

Exercise 5.2 — Backtest the drawdown ladder itself: apply it to the QAE equity curve.
Does de-grossing improve or hurt terminal wealth? (It usually costs return and buys survival —
know which trade you are making.)

Module 6 — Running the loop (week 19–24, then forever)

Goal: operate the system, not the ideas.

The monthly cycle:

Sun/Mon   REFRESH   scripts/fetch_history.py --inc  → prices through Friday
          SCORE     scripts/qae.py                  → IC, weights, today's book
          MONITOR   scripts/monitor.py              → threshold breaches
Rebalance REVIEW    diff new book vs held book; trade only the deltas
          FILE      decision-register rows + prediction-ledger P-calls
Quarterly BACKTEST  re-run walk-forward; retire factors whose IC decayed

Attribution is the point. Each month answer: how much P&L came from (a) the market,
(b) each factor, (c) name-specific luck, (d) costs? A month you cannot attribute is a month
you learned nothing.

Exercise 6.1 — Build a one-page monthly attribution note. Template:
market beta contribution · factor contribution · residual · turnover cost · biggest winner
and loser with the reason.

Exercise 6.2 — At month 6, compute your realized IC (were the top-ranked names actually
your winners?) and your calibration (do 4/5-conviction calls actually beat 2/5 calls?).
track-record is where this lives.

Reference shelf

Books, in reading order
1. Ernie Chan — Quantitative Trading (the fastest orientation; do the code)
2. Grinold & Kahn — Active Portfolio Management (the theory that matters)
3. Antti Ilmanen — Expected Returns (why risk premia exist at all)
4. Andrew Ang — Asset Management: A Systematic Approach to Factor Investing
5. López de Prado — Advances in Financial Machine Learning (read defensively — it is
mostly a catalogue of how backtests lie)
6. Narang — Inside the Black Box (structure of a quant firm, plain language)

Papers to actually read (not cite)
Jegadeesh–Titman 1993 · Fama–French 1993 & 2015 · Carhart 1997 · Asness–Frazzini–Pedersen
2014 (QMJ) · Frazzini–Pedersen 2014 (betting against beta) · Daniel–Moskowitz 2016 ·
McLean–Pontiff 2016 (alpha decay) · Harvey–Liu–Zhu 2016 (…and the Cross-Section of Expected
Returns — the multiple-testing critique that should make you delete half your ideas).

Math you actually need (and the parts you can skip)
- Needed: linear regression and its assumptions · covariance/correlation · z-scores and
ranks · Spearman vs Pearson · t-stats and multiple-testing · compounding and log returns ·
variance of a weighted sum. That is genuinely most of it.
- Useful later: PCA (factor orthogonalization) · Kalman filters (time-varying beta) ·
convex optimization (mean-variance with constraints).
- Skip until L4: stochastic calculus, options pricing theory, deep learning. None of it
helps a daily-bar cross-sectional book, and all of it is a procrastination magnet.

The three rules that matter more than the syllabus

  1. Write the invalidation before the entry. Every time. No exceptions.
  2. Measure before you believe. A factor without an IC number is a story.
  3. Your edge is size and patience, not speed or complexity. You can hold a
    capacity-constrained position that a $50B fund cannot. That is the whole advantage
    (quant-firm-anatomy §3).
